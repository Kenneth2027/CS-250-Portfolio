## Essential Questions — Course Reflections

### How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program? How does creating "user stories" help with this?

Interpreting user needs starts with listening to users directly — not guessing what they want. As the Product Owner, I turned feedback from a customer focus group into user stories in the form "As a [user], I want [goal], so that [benefit]." One focus-group participant said she would never book a tropical trip, which showed that a generic "top ten beaches" list would fail her even though it looks like a reasonable feature on paper. That is what user stories do: they force you to name who benefits, what they need, and why, so the whole team shares the same understanding before code is written. Stories also carry acceptance criteria — clear pass/fail checks — so a tester can verify the feature and the team can tell "done" from "almost done." In practice, a story like "budget-conscious traveler filters by price" gives developers the intent behind the feature instead of a bare instruction, which makes the implementation better and easier to verify.

### How do I approach developing programs? What Agile processes do I hope to incorporate into my future development work?

I approach developing programs as a series of small, working slices rather than one large build. Requirements, design, implementation, and testing repeat in short cycles, and each cycle ends with something demonstrable. In the CS 250 pilot this paid off directly: when the Product Owner changed the travel focus to detox and wellness with the deadline unchanged, the developer (me) absorbed the change by replacing the title, five photos, and captions while deliberately leaving the navigation and layout alone — a bounded update instead of a restart. In future development work I want to keep several practices: a prioritized backlog so the most valuable work goes first; short sprints with a clear sprint goal; testing built in alongside development rather than pushed to the end; daily stand-ups focused on blockers rather than status; and retrospectives that turn lessons into concrete changes for the next iteration. I also want to keep the habit I practiced in the developer role: documenting changes with clear comments, so the code explains not just what changed but why.

### What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?

Being a good team member means keeping your work visible, asking questions early instead of guessing, and respecting your teammates' time and expertise. In this course, the tester's clarifying questions to the Product Owner prevented the whole team from building on a wrong assumption about the interface. My own communication drafts worked best when they were specific: named recipients, numbered requests, and a stated reason for the deadline. A good team member also treats silence as a blocker to raise — not as approval to build on an assumption — and follows up in the right forum (like the Daily Scrum) instead of going silent. Finally, it means valuing shared quality: agreeing up front what "done" means, so finished work is genuinely finished, and being willing to help outside your specialty so the team has no single points of failure. That last lesson comes straight from the Vision Quest case study we analyzed: specialists who cannot cover for each other create the "illusion of progress" — many things started, few finished.
